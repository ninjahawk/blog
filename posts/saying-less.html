Field Notes

We spent two years paying models to think longer. Google just shipped one that thinks 17% less and called it the upgrade.

Output tokens are the serial part of the computation. Everyone kept optimizing the part that was already parallel.

NinjaHawk July 22, 2026

I've been poking at Gemini 3.6 Flash since it showed up yesterday, and there's one number in the announcement that I think basically everyone is going to scroll straight past. Not the price (output down to $7.50 per million from $9), not the March 2026 cutoff, not the Computer Use stuff being built in now. It's this: Google says 3.6 Flash hits the same or better scores than 3.5 Flash while emitting about 17% fewer output tokens to get there. On DeepSWE, one of the long-horizon coding evals, they claim up to 65% fewer.

Read that again, because it's a strange thing to lead a launch with. Part of the pitch for the new model is that it talks less.

I think this is a much bigger deal than a 17% line item makes it look, and to see why you have to remember something that your invoice actively hides from you: an output token and an input token are not the same kind of object.

An output token is not an input token

Here's the thing I always come back to. When you send a prompt, the whole thing goes through the model in one shot. That's the prefill: one big parallel pass, the matmuls are nice and wide, the GPU is happy, and per token it's cheap and fast. That's your input.

Then the model starts generating, and generation is the exact opposite. It's one token at a time, and each token depends on every token before it, and there is genuinely no way around that. Token N+1 cannot exist until token N does. You can't parallelize a sequence against itself. So decode is this slow, autoregressive, memory-bandwidth-bound crawl where the GPU drags the entire KV cache through just to pick one next word, and then does the whole thing again for the next one.

Which means output tokens aren't just the expensive half of the bill. They're the serial half of the computation. They're the part you actually sit there and wait on. It's why the "tokens per second" number everyone quotes is always the decode number and nobody ever quotes prefill. And for agents specifically this is the whole ballgame, because in a loop the output is on the critical path of every single step. The model writes, that becomes a tool call, that comes back, it writes again. The tokens it generates are the thing standing between one step and the next, every hop, all the way down.

So cutting output tokens by 17% is not shaving 17% off some line item. It's shaving it off the one part of the system that flatly refuses to parallelize.

Let me just do the napkin math, it's kind of fun. Say a normal-ish agent run emits 40k output tokens over the whole trajectory: the reasoning, the tool calls, the final diff. Old price, that's $0.36. New price, $0.30. Knock 17% off the count and you're down around 33k, call it $0.25. So ~30% off the output bill, and it's coming from two things stacking at once, cheaper tokens and fewer of them.

But honestly the money is the boring half. The other half is time. Decode at maybe 150 tokens/sec means 40k tokens is something like four and a half minutes of pure generating; 33k is more like three and a half. Doesn't sound like much until you've got a loop doing that thirty times, and then it's the difference between an agent that feels alive and one that feels like it's buffering. Nobody benchmarks "feels like it's buffering," but that's 90% of what running these things is actually like.

(The 65% on DeepSWE I'd take with a grain of salt. It's a vendor number on an eval the vendor chose, and long-horizon coding is exactly where you can find yourself a flattering trajectory. But even a third of that on real work is a lot if you're running agents at any volume.)

The funny part

What's actually strange about this is the direction. For like two years the entire game pointed the other way: get the model to generate more. Chain of thought. Think step by step. Reasoning traces. Test-time compute. The whole regime where you happily let it burn ten thousand tokens muttering to itself before it answers, because more output was, roughly, more capability. That was the trade and it mostly worked.

And we all know how it fails, because we've all watched it. The model gets into a little loop. Re-reads its own reasoning, second-guesses a call it already made, hits the same tool four times with slightly different arguments, writes a whole paragraph about what it's about to do and then does something else entirely. Tons of output, negative marginal information. People started calling it agentic spiraling, which is a good name, because it looks exactly like an anxious person talking themselves in circles.

So 3.6 Flash's pitch, stripped down, is: it does that less. Fewer steps, fewer tool calls, less circling. And, fine, obviously we want that. But it's still a little funny to me that a year ago the marketing arrow was pointing the exact opposite direction and everyone was nodding along to that one too.

What I don't trust yet

Here's the part I don't know, and I don't think the benchmarks can tell me either. There are two completely different ways to get a model to output 17% fewer tokens. One is that it got smarter and stopped wasting them. The other is that you trained it to quit thinking sooner. And the annoying thing is that on most tasks those look identical: same answer, fewer tokens, smaller bill, everyone's happy. The gap only opens up on the tasks that actually needed the extra thinking, where the second model quietly gets it wrong, just faster and cheaper than it used to. Being concise and giving up early produce the exact same token count. The average on the eval can't see which one you paid for.

I don't actually think that's what Google did here, to be clear. The coding scores went up, not down, so at least in aggregate they didn't buy the brevity by trading away correctness. But "in aggregate" is doing a lot of quiet work in that sentence. The place I'd stay nervous is the long tail of the long-horizon stuff, which, surprise, is exactly where that 65% number comes from, and exactly where a model that's learned to decide it's done thinking is the last thing you want in the loop. I'll believe the terseness is free when I've watched one grind through a few hundred hops on something that actually fights back. For now it's a number I like and don't fully trust, which is a fine place to leave it.

The rest of the release, quickly. There's a 3.5 Flash-Lite at $0.30 in / $2.50 out for when you want cheap and don't care about reasoning, and a 3.5 Flash Cyber that's a security-tuned thing locked to governments and "trusted partners," which is a whole separate can of worms. The actual flagship, 3.5 Pro, slipped again, and Google mentioned it's already kicked off its biggest pretraining run yet on Gemini 4 in the same breath, which is generally the kind of thing you bring up specifically because your current flagship is late.

And the thing quietly setting the price floor under all of it: DeepSeek V4's stable weights land July 24, Kimi K3's on the 27th. When there's a comparable model you can just run yourself two days after the paid launch, "we use 17% fewer output tokens" stops being a bullet point and becomes the actual reason to pay. Efficiency is what you sell once you can't sell scarcity anymore.

Anyway. I think the quiet story here is that the thing everybody optimizes is sliding from "tokens" to "tokens you actually needed," and 3.6 Flash is the first model I've seen priced and scored like somebody on the inside finally believes that. Worth watching whether the others follow, and whether the tail holds up when they do.
