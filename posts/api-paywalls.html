I Looked Into How Platforms Are Selling Our Posts

Every platform seems to be somewhere on the same path of training on your posts, charging to read them, and banning you if you get in the way.

NinjaHawk July 12, 2026

I was looking at X's developer pricing, which went into effect in February 2026, and one part of it stuck with me.

ActionPrice
Post read$0.005
Post write$0.015
Post write (with link)$0.20
User read$0.010

The read cap is two million posts a month, and if you need more than that the only option is Enterprise, at roughly $42,000 a month.

So it's half a cent to read one post through the API. That post was written by a user, and the user wasn't paid for it. Under X's terms of service, effective November 15, 2024, that same post comes with a worldwide, royalty-free license for X to train its AI models on it, and for posts there's no opt-out.

Why I started looking at this

I keep seeing roughly the same three things happen.

  1. Use your data to train their model.
  2. Make you pay to access the data.
  3. Ban your account if you interfere with either.

A lot of this is right there in the pricing pages, filings, and moderation decisions. I wanted to put the examples together because the pattern bothered me more once I did.

First, the posts become training data

Reddit. In February 2024 Reddit signed a licensing deal with Google, reported at $60 million per year, which gave Google API access to train Gemini on user posts. Reddit's IPO prospectus disclosed data licensing contracts with an aggregate value of $203 million. A separate deal with OpenAI followed in May 2024.

X. The November 2024 terms of service grant X a license to use posts to train machine learning and AI models "for any purpose." Grok is trained on this. Users outside the EU cannot opt their posts out.

Stack Overflow. In May 2024 Stack Overflow set up an API partnership that gave OpenAI access to its answer corpus, which is sixteen years of volunteer-written answers submitted under a license that gives the platform perpetual rights.

In every one of these cases the content was written by users, for free, before the deal existed. The license terms that made the sale legal were accepted at signup, years earlier, for what was basically a different platform under different owners.

Then it gets expensive to read them

X went through the whole thing.

Date Event
Jan 2023 Third-party clients (Tweetbot, Twitterrific) cut off with no announcement. Days of silence, then a retroactive rule change.
Feb 2023 Free API discontinued. Paid tiers introduced.
2023–2025 Tiers settle at Basic $200/mo, Pro $5,000/mo, Enterprise ~$42,000/mo.
Feb 2026 Pay-per-use becomes the default. $0.005 per post read. No free tier. Basic and Pro closed to new signups.

Reddit announced its API price in April 2023: $0.24 per 1,000 calls. Apollo, the largest third-party client, made 7 billion calls a month, which put Apollo's bill at roughly $1.7 million per month, $20 million per year. Apollo shut down June 30, 2023, and the subreddit blackout that followed didn't change anything.

Meta skipped charging altogether and just removed access. CrowdTangle, the tool journalists and researchers used to track content on Facebook and Instagram, was shut down August 14, 2024. The replacement, Meta Content Library, is restricted to approved academics and nonprofits, and most journalists don't qualify.

For an individual, this means a 10,000-post history costs $50 to read back through the API at $0.005 a post, and your share of the licensing revenue earned from that same history is $0.

And sometimes you can't take them back

In May 2024, Stack Overflow users responded to the OpenAI deal by deleting or overwriting their own highest-voted answers. Moderators reverted the edits and suspended the accounts, some within an hour. Their reasoning was that content, once submitted, is perpetually licensed to the platform, so removing your own answer counts as vandalism.

I think that's the most important fact in all of this, because it means you can't take anything back. The license you granted at signup survives your objection, your account deletion, and in Stack Overflow's case, your attempt to delete the words you wrote.

Enforcement also runs platform-to-platform. Reddit sued Anthropic in June 2025, alleging over 100,000 unauthorized requests to scrape user content. The complaint is about the data being taken without paying Reddit, not about user data being taken in the first place. The users show up in the lawsuit as the property being fought over, and they aren't a party to it.

What I think this means

Looking at X, Reddit, Stack Overflow, and Meta, I keep coming back to the same thing. Access to user-written content has become more restricted or more expensive while that same content has become valuable training data. The details differ by platform, but those are the changes I was trying to understand.

My read on it is that user-generated content became something with a price the moment LLM companies started paying for text, and free APIs were giving that away, so the free APIs went away. The API price is basically the market price of what users write, collected by the platform. I'd say X's $0.005 per read is less a rate limit with a fee attached and more a unit price on other people's writing.

Going forward, I expect the remaining holdouts to follow. I think any platform with a meaningful text corpus and a legal team will end up at the same three steps, because each step makes sense on its own and nothing pushes back. If there are exceptions, I think they'll come from how the platform is built rather than anyone's morals, like federated protocols where the data is public by design, and EU platforms where regulators mandate researcher access. I could be wrong about the timeline, but I'm probably not wrong about the direction.

What I keep coming back to

What bothers me is that you can spend years contributing to a platform, watch that writing become valuable to the platform, and then find yourself paying to access it or unable to remove it. I don't think those decisions were made with the people writing the posts in mind.
