I Built a Stock Index for Pokémon Cards

I made a 500-card index that updates daily, and it's live at poké500.com.

NinjaHawk July 15, 2026

On July 14, 2026 my Pokémon card stock index, the S&Poké 500, closed at 1,074.32. That was down 1.21%, or 13.20 points, from the day before. The basket behind it was worth $253,171.28. 27 cards went up, 22 went down, and 451 didn't change, all based on TCGplayer market prices.

It's a stock index for Pokémon cards that I launched this week at poké500.com. There's one number, one chart, and a table of the 500 cards in it. The name is a parody, the S&Poké 500, ticker SPK500, and it isn't affiliated with S&P Global, Nintendo, The Pokémon Company, or TCGplayer.

Why I made it

I wanted to see what a stock market index for Pokémon cards would actually look like. So I made a price-weighted index of the 500 most valuable English, ungraded Pokémon cards, and set it to start at 1,000. It moves with the combined market price of the basket, so it goes up when cards get pricier and down when the market cools off. I copied the layout from the Google Finance quote page, so you get the big number up top, a gradient area chart with a scrubber, a previous-close reference line, movers, and a searchable table.

How I calculate the number

The index is the sum of the 500 card prices divided by a divisor. At the February 8, 2024 base date the basket was worth $90,695.71 and the divisor was 90.696, which sets the index to exactly 1,000.00. On July 14, 2026 the basket was worth $253,171.28 and the divisor was 235.657, which puts the index at 1,074.32.

The basket value and the divisor are both published in the site's latest.json. The divisor is there for the same reason the Dow has one. Cards fall out of the top 500 and new ones come in, and every time the basket changes the divisor gets adjusted so that the swap itself doesn't move the number, only actual price changes do.

This is really why I bothered with a divisor in the first place. The dollar sum of the basket nearly tripled since February 2024, but almost all of that came from new sets entering the top 500 at high prices. The index takes that out and only reports price movement, which is up 7.4% over the same period.

There are two scope rules. Cards are English and ungraded, priced by TCGplayer's market price for the regular printing. And 1st Edition printings are left out entirely, since they trade too thinly on TCGplayer to get reliable daily prices.

Where the data comes from

Prices are TCGplayer market data via tcgcsv.com, which publishes one snapshot per day (the July 14 snapshot landed at 20:05 UTC). A scheduled job checks every hour, recomputes the index when a new snapshot shows up, and commits fresh latest.json and history.json files. The site itself is static, and if you leave a tab open it polls the JSON every five minutes and rolls over to the new day on its own.

On July 14, 27 cards went up, 22 went down, and 451 stayed the same, which is a pretty normal day. Most of these cards don't trade every day, and TCGplayer's market price is a smoothed estimate rather than the last trade price, so when a thinly traded card does reprice it tends to jump. That day the top gainer was Alakazam E4 Lv.X from Rising Rivals, up 50% to $300, and the top loser was Jolteon Star from Power Keepers, down 22.5% to $1,549.97.

A 50% day on a $300 card is probably just one sale, so the daily changes on individual cards are really noisy. The index level is where that evens out, since you have 500 mostly static prices, a handful of jumps, and in this case a 1.21% move overall.

The chart

History runs from February 8, 2024 to now, which is 129 points. Everything before launch is backfilled from tcgcsv's archive at weekly resolution, and from launch on it records one point per day. The range buttons go from 1W to 5Y, and the shorter ones stay disabled until there's enough daily history to fill them.

So far the line shows the index spent late 2024 underwater, bottoming at 943.14 on October 10, 2024. It recovered through 2025 and hit its all-time high of 1,107.91 on June 18, 2026, and it's drifted down about 3% since then.

How I built the site

It's static HTML, one CSS file, and one vanilla JavaScript file, with no framework, no build step, no dependencies, and no chart library. The chart is hand-drawn SVG. If you tap a row it opens a lightbox with the card image and a link to its TCGplayer page. The page doesn't load any analytics or third-party scripts, and the only external requests are card thumbnails from TCGplayer's CDN.

The domain has the accent in it. poké500.com is a real name you can register because of internationalized domains, and under the hood it's xn--pok500-dva.com. Both go to the same place.

What I think of the results

A lot of the complicated part was already available as daily price data. I put a Dow-style index on top of it, used the archive to backfill two years, and built a quote page around the results.

Taken at face value, it says the top of the raw English card market is up about 7.4% since February 2024 once you take out the new-set churn, with a drawdown through late 2024 and a recovery since then. That's what this particular methodology says from this particular data source.

I think it probably means less than it looks like, though. Price weighting overweights expensive cards. The top 10 cards are 11% of the basket, and the $4,000 Charizard Star counts for 17.7 Psyducks. TCGplayer's market price is an estimate with its own smoothing, and 90% of the cards in the index don't move on a given day, so short windows mostly measure which thin cards happened to trade. I'd say the multi-month trend is probably real, and the daily number is more for entertainment. The site says that in its own footer too: informational and entertainment purposes only, not investment advice.

You can look at the site at poké500.com and it updates once a day, whenever the new market data comes in.
