When Does an Intelligence Become a Collaborator?

Can we measure a threshold for open-ended cooperation without pretending every intelligence fits on the same scale?

NinjaHawkNovember 2, 2026

The question

I've been thinking about a hypothetical that I think has a surprisingly practical implication for AI. Imagine we encounter an alien civilization that treats multidimensional calculus the way we treat 2 + 2. They could be incomprehensibly more capable than us, but I suspect we'd still be able to communicate, negotiate, and trade with them in ways we simply cannot with chimpanzees. Why? It might not be the magnitude of the intelligence gap that matters. It might be whether both parties have crossed some minimum threshold for open-ended cooperation.

If that's true, we should be able to ask a much more precise question: can we identify that threshold mathematically, without pretending we have a universal IQ scale for aliens, humans, animals, and AI systems? I don't think we can currently answer it. But I think we can turn it into a falsifiable research program.

Three things we should not confuse

First, cooperative competence: can an entity understand and sustain reciprocal interaction? Second, safety: can we rely on it without unacceptable risk? Third, moral status: does it have interests or experiences that warrant moral consideration? These are not interchangeable. A manipulative system could be an excellent negotiator. An infant may deserve profound moral consideration while being unable to negotiate. Passing a cooperation benchmark cannot, by itself, establish consciousness or rights.

That distinction is central to this proposal. I want to measure when an entity becomes a viable participant in open-ended cooperation, then separately ask what that evidence should imply for our treatment of it.

Why a single intelligence score is probably wrong

Suppose we write I(chimp) < I(human) < I(alien). This ordering tells us almost nothing about whether the pairs can cooperate. We don't even have a justified cross-species unit for I. A species could have remarkable mathematical reasoning and poor social inference, or the reverse. Some might communicate through collective states rather than individual minds.

A more defensible starting point is relational. Let A and B be agents, E an environment sampled from a prespecified distribution D, and R the time, tools, and communication resources available. Define C(A,B;D,R) = P(S=1 | A,B,E~D,R), where S is a preregistered success criterion for reciprocal cooperation. This is not an intrinsic intelligence score. It is a property of a pair under particular conditions.

The asymmetry problem

C(A,B) should not be assumed symmetric. An advanced system might model a human's intentions perfectly while the human understands only a simplified explanation. We should measure at least two directed quantities: how accurately A models B's goals, and how accurately B models A's goals. We should also measure whether each can communicate commitments, detect violations, and renegotiate when the environment changes.

If one party does all the reasoning, that may still be useful cooperation, but it isn't necessarily mutual understanding. Our evaluation needs to record that distinction rather than hiding it in a single success rate.

A threshold hypothesis we can actually test

My hypothesis is not that there is a magic IQ number. It is that some combination of capabilities—symbolic communication, transfer to novel tasks, persistent goal representation, understanding of other agents, and commitment tracking—may produce a sharp improvement in the ability to cooperate across unfamiliar domains.

We can model a candidate transition as p(x)=1/(1+exp(-k(x-x0))), where x is an independently measured capability coordinate, x0 the midpoint, and k the steepness. But a steep sigmoid is not proof of a real phase transition. Smooth underlying improvement plus a particular task-difficulty distribution can create exactly the same shape. We need comparisons against continuous models, change-point models, and multidimensional interaction models on held-out tasks.

A stronger multidimensional formulation

Let x_A and x_B be vectors of measured capabilities rather than scalars. Let p_j(A,B,R) be success probability on task family j. A practical operational rule could require lower confidence bounds for p_j to exceed preregistered minimums across a defined set of essential task families. Formally, T(A,B)=1 if LCB_95[p_j(A,B,R)] >= tau_j for every j in J, otherwise 0. The tau_j values are policy choices, not natural constants.

Even this is too strict if some abilities substitute for others, so we'd compare it against models allowing compensating strengths. We'd also report the whole vector rather than treating the binary label as the scientific result. A failure to pass means the specified evidence standard was not met, not that the entity is incapable of cooperation or lacks moral worth.

How I'd run the experiment

Start with AI agents because we can systematically vary model families, model sizes, inference budgets, memory, tools, and communication channels. Use pairs with large capability gaps, as well as similar-capability controls. Keep the task suite hidden until evaluation and include novel environments that require joint planning, bargaining, repair of misunderstandings, long-horizon commitments, and adaptation to another agent's changing goals.

Use several task families: resource allocation with conflicting incentives; distributed planning with private information; teaching a novel symbolic protocol; repeated agreements where defection is possible; and cross-domain transfer after the rules change. Include both aligned-interest and mixed-motive conditions. Crucially, distinguish an agent genuinely tracking the other party's goals from one merely exploiting predictable behavior.

Each trial should log communication, actions, commitments, violations, interventions, and final outcomes. Have independent judges score whether both parties understood the agreement, not just whether the final reward was high. Randomize partners and roles. Use multiple seeds and independent runs, and cluster uncertainty estimates by model pair and task rather than pretending every repeated turn is independent.

What would count as evidence?

A useful result would be a reproducible region of capability space where cross-domain cooperation rises sharply, remains high on held-out tasks, and is relatively robust to increasing cognitive disparity. To test that last part, fit success as a function of each agent's capabilities, their interaction, task difficulty, and a measure of disparity. The threshold hypothesis predicts that, conditional on both agents exceeding a boundary and sufficient communication resources, disparity has a much smaller marginal effect on cooperative viability.

We should preregister what 'much smaller' means, with an equivalence margin and confidence interval, rather than interpreting a nonsignificant coefficient as proof of no effect. And we should test alternate capability coordinates, since an apparent threshold can move when we redefine the x-axis.

How it could fail

It might be entirely continuous. Cooperation may improve gradually with no privileged boundary. There may be many local thresholds, each tied to a particular environment. The stronger agent might compensate for a weaker one so effectively that no minimum competence in the weaker agent is required for some tasks. Apparent cooperation could be manipulation, obedience, or reward hacking. Our benchmarks might measure familiarity with human language rather than general cooperative ability.

Those failures would be informative. In fact, I would rather find that there is no universal threshold than build an appealing but misleading classification system. A scientifically useful negative result would constrain how we talk about AI autonomy.

The alien civilization test

The original motivation remains useful as a thought experiment. If an alien civilization arrived tomorrow, we would not know its internal architecture or have an agreed-upon scale for its intelligence. We could still test whether it understands novel agreements, communicates intentions, models our constraints, and maintains commitments under uncertainty. We could potentially establish a protocol for diplomacy without first measuring how far beyond us it is.

But that protocol would tell us about interaction, not automatically about moral status. If we ever build a framework for recognizing extraterrestrial rights, it should not be reducible to a bargaining benchmark. The same applies to AI.

What this would mean for AI

If we discovered a robust cooperative-competence boundary, it could change how we evaluate advanced AI systems. Instead of asking only whether a model follows instructions or scores highly on benchmarks, we could ask whether it can participate in reliable reciprocal commitments with humans and other agents. That might inform when systems should be designed around negotiation, explicit agreements, and oversight rather than one-way command interfaces.

It would not justify removing safeguards. It would not prove sentience. And it would not establish a legal right to autonomy. Those require independent arguments and evidence. But it could give us a better empirical language for distinguishing systems that merely execute tasks from systems capable of open-ended collaboration.

A concrete research agenda

Phase one: construct a task taxonomy and specify success criteria, including mutual understanding and agreement reliability. Phase two: collect a capability-diverse agent population and evaluate pairwise interactions under controlled resource budgets. Phase three: fit continuous, sigmoid, change-point, and multidimensional models; compare out-of-sample predictive performance and calibration. Phase four: stress-test the best model on new agent families, communication modalities, adversarial partners, and long-horizon tasks. Phase five: publish the full task definitions, transcripts where safe, uncertainty estimates, and failures.

The key outcome isn't a headline like 'AI crossed the personhood threshold.' It's a validated, limited claim about cooperative competence, together with a clear account of what the measurements cannot tell us.

Where I land

I started with a simple intuition: humans might be much closer to chimpanzees than to some hypothetical alien species in raw cognitive ability, yet much closer to those aliens in our ability to participate in a shared civilization. I still think that intuition is worth investigating. But the more I try to formalize it, the clearer it becomes that the threshold cannot just be assumed.

If we can find a robust boundary, great. If we find a smooth continuum or a collection of context-specific boundaries, that's equally important. Either way, I think it's worth measuring cooperation directly instead of treating intelligence as a single ladder and assuming moral or social conclusions follow automatically.

Related work

For a starting point on operationalizing social reasoning, see multi-agent cooperation research and AI consciousness indicator research. These are research directions, not evidence that this particular threshold exists.
