Most of your Hacker News karma comes from comment scores only you can see

Hacker News barely changed in twenty years, so I went looking for a hidden reputation score in its plain old code. There isn't one. Karma is mostly built from comment scores the API strips out. But it strips the number and leaves the ranking, so you can recover the order of scores you were told only their authors can see.

NinjaHawk July 22, 2026

Every big platform runs a private score on you. Twitter, TikTok and YouTube all keep some internal number on your account that decides how far your posts travel, and none of them will tell you what it is.

I figured Hacker News might be the exception, because it's old. The site has barely changed in twenty years and adds a feature about once a decade. My guess was that it either never built a hidden score, or built one so long ago that the code would be simple enough to read straight off the public API. So I went looking for mine.

Five fields

The public API hands back everything HN keeps on an account. No key, no login: you ask for a username and get a small JSON object back.

  • id, your username.
  • created, when you signed up.
  • karma, one integer.
  • about, your bio text.
  • submitted, the ID of every post and comment you've ever made.

I pulled a range of accounts and the shape never changes. There's nothing called trust, score or rank, nothing with a suggestive name. The only number that grades you is karma, and karma is already printed next to your username.

So my premise was wrong before I started. HN isn't hiding a reputation model in the page or in old code, because it doesn't have one to hide. Karma is the entire system. I nearly stopped there, but karma didn't behave the way I assumed it would.

Where karma comes from

Karma looks like one clean number. It's the running sum of every vote you've ever received, stories and comments thrown in together and flattened to a single integer. You get the total and nothing underneath it.

You can pull some of that back apart, though not cleanly. HN runs a second public API, the Algolia search index, and it handles the two kinds of item differently. Stories come back with their score attached. Comments come back with everything except the score; the points field just isn't there.

That's on purpose, and it's one of the oldest rules on the site. Every comment has a score, and only the person who wrote it can see it. Everyone else, the API included, gets the comment with the number stripped out. HN's stated reason is to stop people dogpiling votes onto whatever is already ahead.

Story scores are public, so you can add up how much of an account's karma came from submissions and chalk the rest up to comments. I ran that on fourteen well-known accounts. Here are eight of them:

Look at the third column. For almost everyone, the bulk of the karma came from comments. The median across the fourteen was around 80%. rayiner and jerf both clear 98%. These are some of the most trusted names on the site, their standing is built almost entirely out of comments, and every one of those comment scores is invisible to everyone but them.

So the one public measure of how much HN trusts you is built mostly out of numbers nobody is allowed to read, including in some sense the person they belong to, who can only ever see them one at a time.

The attribution is rough and I won't pretend otherwise. Karma is upvotes minus downvotes; I'm treating story-karma as the sum of public story points, which ignores downvotes and assumes one story point is worth one point of karma. sama's ~0% is the estimate hitting its floor: his stories are worth more points than his whole karma total, so the subtraction bottoms out at zero. Read the exact percentages as approximate. The size of the gap isn't up for debate; it's far too large to come out of the math.

What you can see: posting style

What the public side does show is how a person submits, and that varies a lot more than karma lets on. Two numbers cover most of it: the share of posts that clear 100 points (hit-rate), and the share that never make it past 1 point (dead on arrival).

The table falls into two shapes. sama and patio11 post rarely and land often. sama hits 51% of the time; patio11 has 52 submissions across seventeen years, a third of them over 100. edw519 is the opposite: one of the heaviest commenters on the site, he also filed 3,819 submissions, 40% of which died at 1 point and 2% of which cleared 100. Karma shows none of that. Two accounts can sit next to each other on the leaderboard and post nothing alike.

One thing holds across everybody: the dead-on-arrival column never hits zero. Even the accounts that basically live on the front page have a floor of posts that went up and died at 1 point. I found that oddly reassuring.

The two records disagree

Something I wasn't looking for: HN's account record and its search index don't agree. Your account lists everything you've submitted. The index lists everything it can still find. For heavy users the two numbers come apart by tens of thousands of items, and it goes both ways.

dang is the clearest example. His account record lists 83,933 submissions; the search index returns 54,007. Roughly thirty thousand of the moderator's own items sit in his history and never turn up in search. Most of that is probably deleted and dead comments, which stay in the raw history but fall out of the index. It's still an odd result: the person who runs moderation has the least searchable trail on the site, and neither public source is complete by itself.

The order is public

The score is stripped from every comment the API hands back. The order that score produces is not. Ask the Firebase API for a story and you get a kids array: the IDs of its top-level comments. That array is not in time order. It's in HN's display order, and HN displays comments ranked by the score it won't show you.

You can prove this without reading a single score. HN item IDs count up with time, so a chronological list would come back sorted low to high. It doesn't. I pulled the kids array for five threads and lined it up against the same IDs sorted by time:

If the array were chronological the oldest comment would sit at the top and no pair would be out of time order. Instead the oldest comment lands wherever its score puts it, and about half of every thread runs against the clock. That reordering is the hidden score, exposed as a sort.

Take the 2007 Dropbox launch, the thread with the most-quoted comment on the site: the one explaining you could already build Dropbox yourself with rsync and an FTP account. That comment, by BrandonM, is ranked first. Drew Houston's own comment, the oldest top-level reply in the thread, sits at rank 15. Fourteen later comments outscore it. None of those positions carry a number, but the order is exact, and it's public.

So the value of a comment score is private and its rank is not. You can't read that one comment scored some number and another scored a lower one, but you can read, without logging in, that the thread was sorted the first over the second over the rest. Do it for every comment under a story and you've reconstructed the full ranking of scores you were told only their authors can see. It's ordinal, not exact: HN folds comment penalties and a controversy adjustment into the sort, so what you recover is the ranking the site actually uses, not the raw point totals. It works on anyone's comments, which is the part that isn't supposed to be possible.

The one hidden score is yours

There is exactly one genuinely hidden score you can recover as a number, and it's the column Algolia strips out. Every comment you've written carries a point value only you can see, only while logged in, one comment at a time. Nothing sums it for you. But it's your data, sitting behind your own login, so you can walk your entire comment history and add it up yourself. What you get is a number that exists nowhere else: the full distribution of your own comment scores, which HN keeps from you purely by never putting it in one place.

This only works on your own account. Everyone else's comment scores are stripped for you exactly the way yours are stripped for them. So the hidden layer splits in two: your own scores you can recover as numbers, and everyone else's you can only recover as a ranking, never as values.

What this means

I went in expecting to catch an old site quietly grading its users. It isn't doing that. HN never built a reputation model; it decided not to, and the one score it does hide, per-comment points, it hides for a reason it's stated in public.

What's actually there is smaller and, I'd argue, more honest. The public reputation number is an aggregate of private ones. Karma is real, it unlocks real abilities on the site, and for most people it's mostly a comment score whose parts they aren't allowed to see. There's no dossier on you. There's a public running total sitting on top of a long list of numbers that each got removed on the way in.

The removal is less complete than it looks. HN strips the value off every comment and then publishes the order those values produce. So the number is gone and the ranking it feeds is left in the open, on everyone, for free. If you wanted the thing HN actually hides, it turns out most of it is sitting in the sort.

Run it yourself

It's all read-only and needs no account. Two endpoints. hacker-news.firebaseio.com/v0/user/YOURNAME.json returns your karma, account age and full submitted list. hn.algolia.com/api/v1/search?tags=story,author_YOURNAME returns every story with its score; switch to tags=comment and you get every comment with the score missing. Count both, sum the story points, subtract from karma, and you've got your own row of the table. It's about forty lines of Python. Run it on your own username and see how much of your standing came from numbers you were never shown.

For the ranking, drop the account entirely. Ask hacker-news.firebaseio.com/v0/item/STORY_ID.json for any thread, take its kids array, and compare it to the same IDs sorted low to high. Where the two lists disagree, you're looking at the hidden comment scores doing their job. Every gap between them is a score HN decided you shouldn't see, showing through in the one place it forgot to hide.
